Under good conditions a Stratolink balloon reports its position every 20 minutes, and drawing its flight is a matter of joining the dots. But the balloon can’t transmit at night or out of sight of land. This post describes how we can reconstruct the most probable path traveled between known locations.

Joining the dots

Every packet the balloon sends carries a GPS position. Under good conditions, a packet goes out every 20 minutes. Given that the balloon travels around 100 km/hr, this puts consecutive positions only ~30 km apart. That’s close enough that a straight line between them is a reasonably accurate interpretation.

But there are longer stretches between GPS fixes. The payload is solar powered, so it mostly transmits during the day and each night leaves a gap. The balloon’s transmission also needs to be heard by a gateway receiver on the ground. In some areas of the world, particularly over oceans, there are no gateways to hear the message. The balloon only transmits its latest position, so this can leave large stretches of time and distance where the balloon’s path is unknown.

Knowing the path of the balloon is half the fun, so we want to have a way to reconstruct the route that it traveled between two points. The figure below shows the reconstructed path of Stratolink 3. It went eight days without sending a fix as it crossed from New Mexico to Spain.

What we know

For any gap, the problem is the same. We know where the balloon was at the two endpoints, and at what time. We also know roughly how high it was based on the readings from the pressure sensor. The balloon has no motor, so at a steady pressure it goes wherever the air at that pressure goes. Everything below follows from that.

Follow the winds

To find the path between the start and endpoints we need to follow the winds. At float altitude, winds rarely blow in a consistent direction and at constant speed and the balloon will follow in that path. We can use stratospheric wind data from weather models to give an estimate of how the winds transported the balloon.

Unfortunately, wind data is not precisely measurable in the atmosphere, and these wind models only provide an approximation of the balloon’s path. As seen in the figure, naively tracing the wind field doesn’t result in the balloon reaching the right endpoint at the right time.

A little noise, a huge spread

So the winds from the model, and the balloon’s path by extension, may not match reality. How much can that matter? To test, we’ll simulate a set of balloons and give them slight deviations from the wind. We’ll move the wind’s direction by a few degrees, increase or decrease the wind speed by a little bit, or change the balloon’s height within the wind field. We’ll let them loose and see how close they end up.

After simulating these balloons for eight days we see that the potential paths of the balloon stretch across the Atlantic, Europe and North Africa. The median balloon ends 3,590 km from where the balloon really was, and fewer than one in two hundred end within 300 km of it. The errors are small, but a balloon a few degrees off early on can end up on an entirely different path. Small differences grow until the possibilities span an ocean, which is also why weather forecasts give out after a week or so.

Using both ends

It would be easy to conclude that the path can’t be known. But that cloud uses half of what we know. It knows where the balloon started and nothing about where it ended, and the fix at the far end of the gap rules out almost every particle in it. So a gap isn’t a forecasting problem. The question isn’t where the balloon could have gone from the first fix. It’s which paths between the two fixes the winds make most probable.

The blue cloud is the bridge: the same particles and the same winds, held to starting on one fix and ending on the other. Here’s how they’re built.

Meeting in the middle

A forward filter runs a cloud of particles from the fix before the gap, and a backward filter runs another from the fix after it through time-reversed winds. Neither knows about the other end. Each particle carries the same kind of small errors as the cloud above, standing in for everything the model doesn’t capture. A particle that wanders off the high-resolution winds keeps going on coarser ones (more on those below); only one that leaves the stored winds altogether is weighted down. When too few particles in a filter carry most of the weight, the filter is resampled so the cloud never collapses onto a handful of them. For the eight-day gap each filter runs 8,000 particles; shorter gaps settle with 2,000.

The two clouds meet at the middle of the gap and overlap for a window either side of it, eight hours on the eight-day gap. Every forward particle is paired with every backward particle near it, and each pair is weighted by how closely its two halves track each other across the overlap. Two halves that cross at the midpoint heading in different directions pull apart over the window, so the match rewards agreeing on direction as well as position. Inside the window the two halves are blended into one continuous path.

Those pair weights are the answer. Pairing two clouds explores every combination of a forward half and a backward half for the cost of integrating two clouds: on Stratolink 3’s gap, 8,000 forward particles times 8,000 backward ones is 64 million candidate bridges, and about 600,000 of them carry meaningful weight. Together they are the set of routes the winds allow between the two fixes, each weighted by how plausible it is; the dashboard draws from 2,000 of them. The whole reconstruction runs in well under a minute.

Not every gap is eight days. Gaps under 20 minutes are joined with a straight line. Gaps from 20 minutes to six hours get a small version of the same idea: solve for the small speed and heading correction that makes a path driven by the wind land on the next fix, launch 200 particles from each fix, run the forward ones toward the far fix and the backward ones through time-reversed winds toward the near one, weight each by how close it lands, and average. Gaps of six hours and more, overnight gaps included, get the two filters. On Stratolink 3 that is 246 gaps of the first kind, 4 of the second and 4 of the third, and those last 4 hold 92% of the flight.

What a particle assumes

Each particle moves with the GFS wind at its own pressure, interpolated between the surrounding grid points, time slices and pressure levels, in 20-minute steps. Its heading offset and speed factor wander around zero with spreads of 12° and 15% and a memory of about 18 hours. Its pressure starts at the reading from its fix and wanders with an 8 hPa spread and a memory of about two days, around a line between the readings at the two ends of the gap.

Where the winds come from

The winds come from GFS, which runs four times a day. NOAA puts every run in a public S3 bucket with an index of the byte range of each field, so we can fetch just the wind fields we need, for free and without an API key.1 For the reconstruction we read four pressure levels around the balloon’s float, 200, 250, 300 and 350 hPa, from the model’s analyses every three hours; for the forecast, the latest cycle’s hourly winds blended to the float pressure.

Each field is the whole globe, a million grid points, so the winds a balloon needs are cut into a tube: a stack of three-hour slices, each a box at full 0.25° resolution. Around a short gap the box sits on the line between the two fixes. Inside a long gap it follows the winds: before the box is cut, a small pilot cloud of particles is run forward from the fix before the gap and backward from the fix after it, and each box is sized to hold those clouds over the six hours it is read. That matters, because a long gap rarely runs along the line between its fixes. The winds took Stratolink 3 to the middle of the Atlantic in two days and then held it for four in a slow loop west of the Azores; a tube laid along the straight line to Spain would have been somewhere else entirely by the second day. Around every box the cube also keeps the wind at a coarser 1.5° over most of the hemisphere, so a particle that wanders out of the box still rides real winds. For Stratolink 3 the reconstruction cube is about 22 MB and the hourly forecast cube 1.8 MB.

Checking it

The first thing to check on any reconstruction is whether the balloon behaves like a balloon. For every step of every bridge, measure the angle between the direction it moved and the wind at that place and time. If the particles could fly across the flow, this is where it would show. On Stratolink 3’s eight-day gap, nine steps in ten are within 20° of the wind, about the same as the forecast ensemble shows, and the bridges move at the wind’s speed.

The other checks are about stability: how closely the forward and backward clouds meet, and how far the drawn line moves when the whole thing is rerun. On the eight-day gap the clouds meet within about 45 km of each other and the line moves by about 10 km. If the winds can’t connect the two ends of a gap at all, the reconstruction says so instead of drawing a line. And the path of the winds alone, from earlier, is a check too. With no randomness at all, the GFS winds take the same route the bridges do, up the jet over Newfoundland, around the loop near the Azores and into Iberia from the southwest, and run backward from Spain they trace it too. The bridges don’t invent a route to connect the fixes. They take the one the winds already offer and bend it by small amounts until it lands on both ends. The eight-day gap is explained by the winds at the balloon’s own altitude.

One line from 2,000

The dashboard draws one line, so the bridges have to be boiled down to one. When they form a single bundle, the line is simply their average position at each hour.

That isn’t always safe. If the bridges split, say around a patch of slow air, the average of two routes runs between them, through places the balloon never was. So the reconstruction checks: every two hours it compares how many bridges lie within 300 km of the average with how many lie within 300 km of the center of the biggest bundle. If the average ever holds fewer than half as many, the routes have split, and the line follows the biggest bundle instead. On Stratolink 3 no gap splits, and the line moves by under 20 km when the whole job is rerun.

That line is the center of the routes the winds allow, not a measurement. In the middle of Stratolink 3’s gap, 90% of the bridges fall within 500 to 800 km of it. The line is our best single guess through a corridor that wide, and showing the corridor is the thing I most want to change next.

Where it goes next

The forward forecast is the same integration pointed at the future, with one change. Instead of perturbing a single wind field, each path rides a different model run. NOAA’s Global Ensemble Forecast System, GEFS, publishes 31 members, each started from slightly different initial conditions, and its machine-learning counterpart AIGEFS publishes another 31.2 We cut a tube for every member and integrate the balloon from the last fix through each one, which gives 62 paths that disagree exactly as much as the weather models do.

The line the dashboard draws is the per-hour median of the members, and the forecast ends at the first hour where the 75th-percentile distance of the members from that median passes 2,000 km. Past that point the members are spread across a continent and the median isn’t predicting anything. From Stratolink 3’s last fix near Málaga on May 29, the members ran east across the Mediterranean, Asia and the Pacific, and their spread crossed 2,000 km on June 9, ten and a half days out, over Utah.

A balloon that stops reporting for good turns the stretch from its last fix to now into a forecast as well, and the dashboard draws it as one. Stratolink 3 never sent another position; the route at the top of Teddy’s June post came from an earlier experiment with this machinery, pushed on for weeks toward a guessed endpoint. The forecast now stops when the models stop agreeing, which is the honest place to stop.

The plumbing

All of this runs on a GitHub Actions runner, four times a day. A run starts by deciding which balloons need work. A balloon whose last fix is more than 15 days old and whose stored forecast already ends at its divergence point cannot change, so it is skipped, and when the fleet is quiet the run plans nothing, downloads nothing and exits. When a balloon is flying, the runner pulls the wind fields it needs from NOAA’s bucket by byte range, builds the two cubes plus a tube per ensemble member, runs the reconstruction and the forecast, and writes one JSON file per balloon, about half a megabyte, to Vercel Blob. The dashboard reads that file and draws.

Each bridge is cached by a hash of its two fixes, the pressure level and the method version, so a rerun only recomputes the gaps that changed. For Stratolink 3 the whole compute, reconstruction and forecast together, takes under a minute on the runner. The ingest takes longer, and almost all of it is waiting on S3 for the member cubes; when a few members don’t arrive inside the step’s time budget, the forecast runs on the ones that did.

Where the code is

Everything is in the Stratolink repository, under web/. The ingest is scripts/gfs_ingest.py, with gefs_ingest.py and aigefs_ingest.py for the ensemble members. The cube format and wind sampling are in lib/wind/windCube.ts, the short and medium gaps in lib/wind/pathReconstruction.ts, the long-gap filters and the drawn line in lib/wind/pathReconstructionLongGap.ts, and the forecast in lib/wind/monteCarloForecast.ts. The schedule is .github/workflows/gfs-ingest.yml.

What’s next

Three things, in the order I think they matter.

Show the corridor. We have 2,000 plausible routes for every long gap and draw one. I’ve prototyped ribbons, sample bundles and daily uncertainty ellipses on the dashboard map, and none of them is on the site yet, mostly because a band several hundred kilometers wide across the Atlantic has to be drawn carefully to read as “somewhere in here” rather than as noise.

Calibration. The spreads each particle carries come from one flight, which had only a handful of clean fix pairs to learn from. Replaying more flights, and the stretches of them where we did get positions, should pin down how far a balloon really strays from the model wind.

More fixes. Everything here gets easier when the balloon talks more often, and Teddy’s v1 firmware rejects stale fixes so that a packet with a position means a position. The Baja leg of Stratolink 3’s flight, where we had a position every few minutes, needed none of this.

The positions we did receive, and the line through them, are on Stratolink 3’s flight. Shepherd’s Baja Run covers the first two days, and Teddy’s Looking for Stratolink 3 picks up after the last packet.
